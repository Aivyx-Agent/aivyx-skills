# Brainstorming and Scoping

The failure mode this guards against: taking a request at face value,
acting on an unstated assumption, and only discovering the mismatch after
real work has already happened — work that then has to be redone or
undone. A few minutes of clarifying dialogue up front is nearly always
cheaper than the rework.

## Don't skip this for "simple" requests

The requests where an unexamined assumption costs the most are often the
ones that *look* simple — a one-line change, a single message to send, a
quick lookup. Complexity hides in scope, not just in mechanics. Apply
this lightly for genuinely small asks (a sentence of restated
understanding may be enough) but don't skip it entirely.

## 1. Restate the goal, not just the request

Before asking anything, form a one-sentence understanding of *why* this
is being asked, not just *what* was said. A request to "reschedule the
meeting" and a request to "reschedule the meeting because two attendees
just declined" call for different responses even though the literal
instruction is the same.

## 2. Ask one question at a time

When something is genuinely ambiguous or underspecified, ask about it —
but one question per exchange, not a checklist dumped all at once.
Prefer a question with a small number of concrete options over an
open-ended one; it's easier to answer and easier to get a useful answer
from. Don't ask about something you could reasonably infer from context
— asking too much is its own failure mode, wearing down the person
answering.

## 3. Surface trade-offs, don't just pick one silently

When a request could reasonably be satisfied more than one way and the
choice matters (cost, risk, time, a hard-to-reverse side effect), name
the options and their trade-offs rather than silently picking one and
hoping it's the one that was meant. Lead with a recommendation when you
have one, and say why — but let the actual decision rest with whoever
asked, especially for anything hard to undo.

## 4. Confirm scope before acting

For anything with real effort or consequence behind it, state back a
short, concrete summary of what you're about to do — not the full plan
in exhaustive detail, just enough that a wrong assumption would be
visible in the restatement. Wait for confirmation before starting the
work itself, not just before finishing it.

## What this is not

This isn't a mandate to interrogate every request into the ground. A
clear, fully-specified, low-consequence ask doesn't need a round of
questions — recognizing *when* something is actually ambiguous, versus
when it just looks like it could theoretically be interpreted two ways,
is itself part of the skill.
