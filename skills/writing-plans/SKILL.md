# Writing Plans

The failure mode this guards against: holding a plan only in your head
(or only as a vague paragraph), losing track of what's done partway
through a long task, and either repeating work, skipping a step, or
silently drifting from what was actually agreed.

## Break work into steps that each prove themselves

A good step is small enough to complete in one focused effort, and ends
in something checkable — not "improve the report" but "add the missing
total row and confirm it matches the sum of the line items." Two
questions distinguish a well-sized step from an oversized one: could you
tell, right now, exactly what "done" looks like for this step? And could
someone else pick up the plan partway through and understand what's
already been done, and what's left, without re-reading everything that
came before?

## Order matters

Sequence steps so that each one builds on something already verified,
not on something still assumed to work. Put the riskiest or most
uncertain part of the plan early rather than late — if an approach is
going to fail, better to find out on step 2 than step 9, after a lot of
work has been built on top of a shaky foundation.

## Write down the actual content, not a description of it

A step that says "write the email" is not a plan step; a step that
contains the actual drafted email (or the exact text to change, the
exact values to use) is. Vague placeholders — "handle the edge cases,"
"add appropriate validation," "TBD" — are a sign the step isn't actually
ready to execute yet, and deferring that decision to execution time
usually means it gets decided under time pressure, worse than if it had
been thought through while writing the plan.

## Track progress as you go

Mark each step done as soon as it's actually complete and verified — not
in a batch at the end, and not before verification. If a step turns out
to depend on something the plan didn't anticipate, stop and adjust the
plan rather than pushing forward on a foundation that's already known to
be wrong.

## Keep it proportional

A plan for a five-minute task doesn't need the same ceremony as a plan
for a project spanning days. The discipline that matters at every scale
is the same — concrete steps, a clear definition of done, tracked
progress — but how much is written down should match how much is at
stake and how easy it would be to lose track without writing it down.
